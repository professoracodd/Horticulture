Section 1

Where Does a Plant's Mass Come From?

Common Misconception

95% of a tree's physical mass comes directly from carbon dioxide (CO₂) absorbed from the air.

The Reality

While almost all dry plant biomass originates from photosynthetic processes rather than the soil, atmospheric CO₂ accounts for only about 50% of that mass. The remaining elements come from water and soil minerals. Soil provides anchorage and essential mineral cofactors, but over 96% of the raw elements that build plant tissue come from water and the atmosphere.

When plant tissue is completely dehydrated to remove all free liquid water, the remaining solid structural material consists of three organic polymers: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. These are built from just three elements: Carbon, Oxygen, and Hydrogen.

Element % Dry Mass Primary Source Role in the Plant
Carbon (C) 48% to 50% Atmosphere (CO₂) Fixed during the Calvin cycle; forms the backbone of all organic molecules.
Oxygen (O) 42% to 44% Water (H₂O) and Atmosphere Incorporated during carbon fixation; structural component of carbohydrates.
Hydrogen (H) 6% Soil Water (H₂O) Extracted during the light reactions of photosynthesis via water splitting (photolysis).
Minerals and Nitrogen 1% to 4% Soil Solution Absorbed by roots; used for enzymes, chlorophyll, DNA, and osmotic regulation.
Section 2

Light: Spectrum and Intensity

Plants do not use all wavelengths of sunlight equally. The range of light usable for photosynthesis is called Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR), spanning 400 nm to 700 nm. Within this range, different wavelengths produce very different biological responses.

Blue Light -- 400 to 500 nm

Chlorophyll a has a primary absorption peak near 430 nm. Blue light drives the highest photosynthetic efficiency and stimulates vegetative leaf development and stomatal opening.

Green Light -- 500 to 600 nm

Largely reflected or transmitted through the leaf canopy. Green light is poorly absorbed by chlorophylls, resulting in low photosynthetic efficiency at these wavelengths.

Red Light -- 600 to 700 nm

Highly absorbed by Chlorophyll a and b. Red wavelengths activate phytochrome receptors, regulating the transition to flowering, fruiting, and photoperiod responses.

Light Intensity and Growth Rate: Intensity determines total energy throughput. At low levels (below 800 lux), light limits the photosynthetic rate. As intensity climbs toward direct sunlight (5,000 to 10,000 lux), daily carbon assimilation rises until the plant reaches its light saturation point. More photons mean more photosynthesis -- up to a ceiling.
Section 3

Temperature: Thresholds and Freezing Mechanics

Biochemical reactions have temperature windows. The optimal zone for most crop species ranges between 65°F and 85°F, where photosynthetic enzymes operate at peak efficiency without thermal degradation. Outside this window, growth slows or stops entirely.

Temperature Range Plant Response Status
Below 28°F Hard frost -- cellular damage through osmotic desiccation or intracellular ice formation Critical
28°F to 32°F Soft frost -- partial damage, some recovery possible depending on species Stress
32°F to 65°F Cold stress -- enzymatic slowdown, reduced growth rate Sub-optimal
65°F to 85°F Optimal growth -- enzymes at peak velocity, full photosynthetic capacity Optimal
Above 86°F to 95°F Heat stress -- photosynthetic enzyme complexes destabilize, respiration outpaces fixation Stress
Above 95°F Extreme heat -- protein denaturation, rapid cellular breakdown Critical

How Freezing Actually Damages Plant Cells

Freezing damage is not simply a matter of ice forming inside cells. The mechanism depends on how quickly temperatures drop.

Slow Freezing -- Extracellular Ice

Ice forms first in the spaces outside the cell wall, where fluids are more dilute. This lowers external vapor pressure, pulling water out of the cell interior by osmosis. The cell undergoes severe dehydration and shrinkage, crushing organelles and tearing the plasma membrane. The primary damage is desiccation, not direct puncture.

Rapid Freezing -- Intracellular Ice

When temperatures drop too quickly, water cannot leave the cell fast enough. Ice crystals form inside the cytoplasm and vacuole, growing internally and physically rupturing membranes. This is typically lethal and irreversible.

Section 4

Soil Water, Aeration, and Microbiology

Soil pore space is a shared volume between liquid water and air. In healthy, uncompacted soil, pore space consists of approximately 25% water and 25% air. Because water and air occupy the same pores, their relationship is directly inverse: as one increases, the other decreases proportionally.

The Microbial Shift

When soil oxygen drops due to waterlogging or heavy surface compaction, the microbial community undergoes a phase shift. Aerobic microbes that support plant health go dormant. Anaerobic microorganisms take over and carry out denitrification, converting nitrate into nitrogen gases (N₂O and N₂) that escape into the atmosphere. They also generate compounds like hydrogen sulfide that are directly toxic to root tissue. The plant loses access to nutrients that are physically present in the soil but chemically unavailable or actively harmful.

Section 5

The Sigmoid Growth Curve

Plant biomass accumulation over a lifecycle is not linear. Total growth follows a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve, reflecting the shifting balance between structural expansion and reproductive allocation. The graph below shows biomass and nutrient uptake as a percentage of total over time -- nitrogen uptake mirrors biomass production because the plant is building new tissue at the same rate it is absorbing nutrients.

Phase 1
Seedling
Slow start. Relies on seed reserves. Small leaf area limits daily carbon capture.
Phase 2
Vegetative
Rapid exponential climb. All energy goes to leaves, stems, and roots. Each new leaf compounds the next.
Phase 3
Flowering
Begins on the shoulder of the curve while growth is still occurring. Energy redirects to reproductive organs.
Phase 4
Seed Development
Structural growth nearly stops. Nitrogen and phosphorus mobilize from leaves into developing seeds.
Phase 5
Maturity
Curve plateaus near 100%. Seed fill complete. Nutrient uptake from soil essentially stops.
A critical point about the reproductive transition: Flowering does not begin after the S-curve flattens. It begins while the curve is still on its upper incline -- the decelerating shoulder. The plant is still growing structurally when it initiates reproduction, but the rate of new biomass production slows sharply as energy and nutrients are redirected toward flowers, fruit, and seeds. This is why vegetative growth produces far more total biomass than reproductive growth: the vegetative phase is when the plant operates as a full-capacity biomass factory.
