Section 1

Where Does a Plant's Mass Come From?

Common Misconception

95% of a tree's physical mass comes directly from carbon dioxide (CO2) absorbed from the air.

The Reality

While almost all dry plant biomass originates from photosynthetic processes rather than the soil, atmospheric CO2 accounts for only about 50% of dry mass. The remaining elements come from water (H2O) and soil minerals. Soil provides physical anchorage and essential mineral cofactors, but over 96% of the raw elements that construct plant tissue originate from water and the atmosphere.

When plant tissue is completely dehydrated to remove all free liquid water, the remaining solid structural material consists of three primary organic polymers: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. These are built entirely from Carbon, Oxygen, and Hydrogen.

Element % Dry Mass Primary Source Role in the Plant
Carbon (C) 48% to 50% Atmosphere (CO2) Fixed during the Calvin cycle; forms the structural backbone of all organic molecules.
Oxygen (O) 42% to 44% Water (H2O) and Atmosphere Incorporated during carbon fixation; vital structural component of carbohydrates.
Hydrogen (H) 6% Soil Water (H2O) Extracted during the light reactions of photosynthesis via photolysis (water splitting).
Minerals & Nitrogen (N) 1% to 4% Soil Solution Absorbed by roots; essential for enzymes, rubisco, chlorophyll, DNA, and osmoregulation.
Section 2

Light: Spectrum and Intensity

Plants do not utilize all wavelengths of sunlight equally. The range of light usable for photosynthesis is designated as Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR), spanning 400 nm to 700 nm. Within this band, distinct wavelengths trigger specialized biological responses.

Blue Light • 400 to 500 nm

Chlorophyll a exhibits a primary absorption peak near 430 nm. Blue light drives peak photosynthetic efficiency, promotes compact vegetative growth, and stimulates stomatal opening.

Green Light • 500 to 600 nm

Largely reflected or transmitted through the upper canopy, giving leaves their green appearance. While green penetrates deeper into thick canopies, photosynthetic efficiency is lowest here.

Red Light • 600 to 700 nm

Highly absorbed by both Chlorophyll a and b (peak at 660 nm). Red photons trigger phytochrome photoreceptors, signaling the transition into flowering and reproductive stages.

Light Intensity & Daily Biomass Accumulation: While spectrum determines efficiency, intensity dictates total photon availability. Below 800 lux, photosynthesis is photon-limited. As intensity increases toward direct sunlight (5,000 to 10,000 lux), carbon assimilation climbs steadily until reaching the plant's light saturation point.
Section 3

Temperature: Thresholds and Freezing Mechanics

Enzyme-catalyzed biochemical reactions operate within strict thermal bounds. The optimal window for most temperate crop species ranges between 65°F and 85°F, where photosynthetic enzymes achieve maximum kinetic rate without structural degradation.

Temperature Range Plant Response Status
Below 28°F Hard frost — cellular lethality via osmotic desiccation or intracellular ice formation Critical
28°F to 32°F Soft frost — extracellular ice begins forming; partial injury depending on freeze duration Stress
32°F to 65°F Cold stress — enzymatic deceleration, suppressed membrane fluidity, slow nutrient uptake Sub-optimal
65°F to 85°F Optimal growth — peak rubisco velocity, optimal stomatal conductance and respiration balance Optimal
86°F to 95°F Heat stress — photorespiration increases sharply; cellular respiration outpaces carbon fixation Stress
Above 95°F Extreme heat — irreversible protein denaturation, membrane leakage, stomatal shutdown Critical

How Freezing Damaging Plant Cells

Freezing damage is rarely caused by ice crystals physically puncturing cells from the outside. Rather, it is an osmotic water-loss catastrophe determined by cooling speed:

Slow Freezing — Extracellular Ice

Ice crystals form first in the dilute fluid between cell walls. Because pure ice has lower vapor pressure than liquid cytoplasm, water is drawn out of the cell via osmosis. The cell suffers lethal desiccation, membrane collapse, and plasmolysis. Desiccation—not ice puncture—is the killer.

Rapid Freezing — Intracellular Ice

During an instantaneous hard freeze, intracellular water cannot exit through the membrane quickly enough. Crystals nucleate directly inside the cytoplasm and vacuole, tearing the lipid bilayer and causing immediate, irreversible lysis.

Soil Water, Aeration, and Microbiology

Soil pore space is a fixed volume shared between liquid water and air. In healthy, structured soil, pore space consists of approximately 25% water and 25% air. Because they occupy the same physical voids, water and air have an inverse relationship: as moisture rises, air is displaced.

The Microbial Phase Shift

When soil oxygen drops below critical levels due to saturation or heavy compaction, the microbial ecosystem undergoes a complete shift. Beneficial aerobic bacteria enter dormancy. Facultative and obligate anaerobic microbes proliferate, initiating denitrification: converting valuable plant-available nitrate (NO3−) into nitrous oxide (N2O) and inert nitrogen gas (N2) that off-gasses into the air. They also generate reduced compounds like hydrogen sulfide (H2S) that actively poison root tissue.

The Sigmoid Growth & Nitrogen Uptake Curve

Plant biomass accumulation over a lifecycle is non-linear. Total accumulation follows an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve. The diagram below shows total cumulative Crop Nitrogen (N) uptake over time—which perfectly tracks physical dry biomass because tissue construction and nutrient assimilation occur in lockstep.

Phase 1
Seedling
Slow establishment. Relies on cotyledon reserves. Small leaf area restricts total daily photon interception.
Phase 2
Vegetative
Exponential climb. Maximum structural leaf and stem expansion. Each new leaf compounds future photosynthetic output.
Phase 3
Flowering
Initiates on the decelerating shoulder of the curve. Resources begin redirecting toward floral meristems.
Phase 4
Seed Fill
Structural growth drops near zero. Nitrogen and carbohydrates mobilize from leaves into developing grains/seeds.
Phase 5
Maturity
Plateau near 100%. Seed maturation complete. Active root uptake and photosynthesis cease.
Key Insight — The Reproductive Shift: Flowering does not wait until biomass accumulation stops. It begins on the upper inflection shoulder of the S-curve. Because energy and absorbed nitrogen are redirected from structural expansion toward reproductive sinks, the rate of new vegetative biomass slows sharply. This explains why the vegetative phase accounts for the vast majority of physical canopy growth.
