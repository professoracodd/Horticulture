Educator Cheat Sheet

For Teachers Only

This reference gives you the scoring mechanics at a glance, classroom-tested tips, and a verified high-scoring food combination for every region and season — generated by running the game's actual scoring engine, not guessed. Use it to check student answers quickly, unstick a group that's hunting blind, or seed a class discussion about why some regions are structurally harder to "perfect" than others.

How Scoring Works
10
Picks per season
Students choose up to 10 foods/supplements each season. Most winning combos in this guide use only 4-6.
23
Tracked nutrients
14 macro/micronutrients (calories, protein, vitamins, minerals) plus 9 essential amino acids, each scored 0-3 per season.
<2
Deficiency threshold
Any nutrient totaling below 2 (of 3) flags as a deficiency; below 1 flags as critical and carries a bigger life-expectancy penalty.
−1.5
Region penalty
Regions flagged low on natural iodine or vitamin D (see each region card) subtract 1.5 from that nutrient's total every season.

Critical Deficiency

Life expectancy isn't pure percentage — severity matters. A season with critical deficiencies (value under 1) is penalized more heavily than one with only mild shortfalls, even at the same overall score. The floor is 50; a flawless, near-100% diet in every season can push past 100.

Classroom Tips
  • 1Point students to the Nutrient Guide before they guess. Every food category has a "Nutrient Guide" button showing exactly which foods cover which nutrients — it's an open-book tool by design, not a hidden mechanic. Struggling groups usually haven't opened it yet.
  • 2One protein-dense pick beats five vegetables. Eggs, fish, dairy, and legumes are the only items that move the amino acid dials — an all-produce selection will always show amino acid deficiencies no matter how many items are chosen.
  • 3Use Loma Linda to teach "no perfect answer." It's the only region that structurally cannot reach 100% (see its card below) — a great prompt for discussing real dietary trade-offs rather than "gaming" the system.
  • 4Watch for over-selection. Since students can pick up to 10 items, some will "spray and pray" rather than think about coverage. Ask them to justify each pick's nutrient contribution — most winning combos here use half the budget or less.
  • 5Compare life expectancy, not just the percentage. Two students can land near the same nutritional score but very different life-expectancy numbers if one had critical (vs. mild) deficiencies — a good jumping-off point for discussing severity vs. frequency of deficiency.
  • 6Season-swap discussion. Have students compare their Spring vs. Winter picks for the same region — most regions require a genuinely different food strategy season to season, mirroring real seasonal eating patterns.
