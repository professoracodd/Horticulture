Plant Cells: The Basic Unit

Every plant is built from cells that are eukaryotic, meaning each one holds a membrane-bound nucleus and a set of organelles that perform specific jobs, similar to how organs work in the human body. A plant cell's organelles include the cell wall, plasma membrane, nucleus, nucleolus, cytoplasm, vacuole, ribosomes, golgi body, endoplasmic reticulum, and mitochondrion. The chloroplast, which carries out photosynthesis, is unique to plant cells.

What Is Plant Growth?

Growth happens through two basic mechanisms working together:

  • Cell division: an increase in the number of cells.
  • Cell elongation: an increase in the size of individual cells.

Both processes are directed by chemical signals the plant produces itself, its hormones.

Phytohormones & Directional Growth

Phytohormones are chemicals produced by plant cells that regulate cellular processes, either near where they're made or after being transported to other organs. Two key players are auxins and cytokinins.

Tropisms

Phototropism is how a plant grows with respect to light; gravitropism is how it grows with respect to gravity. Auxin drives both, but in opposite directions depending on the organ:

Apical dominance: auxin produced at the shoot tip keeps axillary (side) buds dormant. Pruning removes the source of auxin, which is why cutting a stem's tip triggers bushier, lateral growth.

The Six Stages of Growth

All flowering plants move through the same six stages:

Germination

Germination is the process by which a seed breaks dormancy and sends out its first roots and shoots. Seeds are typically sown at a depth 2 to 3 times their own size. Inside a seed, the embryo is protected by a seed coat and fueled by stored food, either an endosperm (in albuminous seeds, mostly monocots) or reserves already absorbed into the cotyledon(s) (in exalbuminous seeds, mostly dicots). Dicots emerge with two cotyledons; monocots keep their single cotyledon below ground.

The sequence: the seed imbibes water and swells, the seed coat cracks, the radicle (first root) emerges, then the hypocotyl ("below the cotyledons") pushes up and breaks the soil surface. That is the cue to move seedlings out of a germination chamber. Finally the cotyledons emerge and the plant can begin photosynthesizing on its own. Seedlings that stretch unusually tall at this stage are usually short on light.

Seedling & Vegetative Growth

As the seedling matures, its cotyledons wither, and the epicotyl ("above the cotyledons") stretches upward. Once two sets of true leaves have replaced the cotyledons, seedlings are ready to transplant out of their plug trays.

Meristems: where new growth originates

A meristem is a zone of undifferentiated cells that divide, differentiate, and elongate. The shoot apical meristem (SAM) and root apical meristem (RAM) drive primary growth, extending the tips of roots and shoots and producing new leaves and flowers. Lateral meristem drives secondary growth, widening the plant and building vascular tissue.

Vegetative growth itself is characterized by heavy growth of roots and shoots. Dicots typically develop a tap root system; monocots develop a fibrous root system. Leaves show parallel matching patterns: monocot leaves have parallel veins, dicot leaves have netted veins. Leaves may be simple (one blade) or compound (divided into leaflets along a rachis, with no bud in the leaflet axils), and are arranged along the stem as opposite, alternate, or whorled.

Reproductive Growth

This stage is marked by the growth of flowers, fruits, and seeds.

Flower Anatomy Quiz Alert

The petals (together, the corolla) attract pollinators; the sepals (together, the calyx) protect the bud before it opens. The pistil, the female structure, is made of the stigma (receives pollen), style (the stalk holding it), and ovary (holds the ovules/seeds). The stamen, the male structure, is made of the anther (produces pollen) and filament (its supporting stalk). A complete flower has all four whorls; a perfect flower has both petals and sepals.

Pollination & Fertilization

Pollen moves from anther to stigma by insects, wind, animals, or self-pollination, either within the same flower or plant (self) or between different plants (cross). During fertilization: the pollen grain germinates, the egg is fertilized and becomes the embryo, and the polar nuclei are fertilized and become the endosperm.

Senescence

As a plant senesces, it pulls back nutrients and compounds, redirecting them into dormancy or into seeds, while preparing for the death of that structure. Cell structures are dismantled and reallocated; some compounds increase in production while others break down at different rates. This shift in chemistry is visible in pigments: green chlorophyll fades, revealing carotenoids, flavonols, and anthocyanins, part of a much broader family of plant phytochemicals (polyphenols, terpenoids, alkaloids, and more) that give plants their color, flavor, and defenses. Eventually, hormones including auxin, ethylene, and abscisic acid trigger leaf abscission, the shedding of the leaf, which leaves behind a leaf scar.

Dormancy

Dormancy is slowed or inactive growth. It can affect an entire plant, its buds, or its seeds. On a dormant branch, every point where a leaf once grew leaves behind a node, bordered by internodes, with lateral buds, leaf scars, and lenticels marking the previous season's growth, capped by a terminal bud.

How Long Do Plants Live?

  • Annuals complete their entire life cycle in one year (seed → vegetative growth → reproductive growth → death/seed), typically propagated by seed. Example: calendula.
  • Biennials take two years: year one is vegetative growth and dormancy; year two brings reproductive growth and death. Example: carrots, which store nutrients in their taproot over winter to flower in year two.
  • Perennials live three or more years, cycling through vegetative growth, reproductive growth, and dormancy annually, and may be herbaceous or woody. Example: agave, a monocarpic perennial that flowers once, at the end of its life.
